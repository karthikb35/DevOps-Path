Monitoring & Observability
The difference between a 5-minute incident and a 5-hour incident is almost always the quality of your observability
🔭

Monitoring tells you that something is broken. Observability tells you why — from outside the system, by examining its outputs, without adding new code. These are distinct capabilities requiring different data. The three pillars (metrics, logs, traces) answer different questions. Together, correlated by a shared trace ID, they reduce mean-time-to-resolution from hours to minutes.

1 · The Three Pillars

🧠
Mental Model — Three Different Instruments for the Same System

A metrics dashboard is a car's dashboard — it shows speed, fuel, temperature at a glance. Logs are the car's event recorder — "warning: oil low at 14:23:07". Traces are the GPS route replay — "the journey from A to B took 847ms; 820ms was spent on the highway (Stripe API)." You need all three to understand a complex journey. The metric tells you you're late; the trace tells you where the bottleneck was; the log tells you why the highway was slow.

PillarQuestion it answersFormPrimary tool
MetricsWhat is happening right now at system level?Time-series numbersPrometheus, Datadog, CloudWatch
LogsWhat happened and what was the context?Timestamped event records (JSON)ELK Stack, Loki, Splunk
TracesHow did this specific request travel through the system?Spans with parent-child relationshipsJaeger, Zipkin, Datadog APM
🔎
Correlation: the superpower that makes all three 10× more valuable

The three pillars become exponentially more powerful when they are correlated via a shared trace_id:

  1. Metric alert fires: p99 latency >500ms on payment-service
  2. Dashboard shows: spike started at 14:23 UTC
  3. Jump to logs: filter service=payment-service timestamp=14:23
  4. Log shows: Stripe timeout errors, trace_id=abc123def456
  5. Jump to trace abc123def456: Stripe API call took 820ms of the 847ms total
  6. Root cause: Stripe API latency spike. Fix: circuit breaker with fallback

Time to root cause with correlation: 5 minutes. Without: 2+ hours of log grepping. This is why every log entry must include trace_id, and every trace must be linkable to metrics.

2 · SLOs, SLIs, SLAs — The Language of Reliability

🧠
Mental Model — The Budget You're Allowed to Spend on Failures

Your SLO says "99.9% availability." That means you're allowed 0.1% failure = 43.8 minutes/month. This is your error budget. You can deploy risky features while you have budget. When your budget is exhausted, you stop shipping features and focus on reliability until the budget resets. This is not punitive — it's a rational model that trades feature velocity for stability automatically, without politics or escalations.

SLA (Service Level Agreement)
Contractual commitment to customers. "We guarantee 99.9% availability. If we breach this, you receive credits." The external-facing promise.
SLO (Service Level Objective)
Internal target, stricter than SLA. "Our internal target is 99.95% availability." The buffer between SLO and SLA gives you time to react before breaching the SLA.
SLI (Service Level Indicator)
The actual measurement used to evaluate the SLO. "Current availability: 99.97% (measured over the last 30 days)."
Error Budget
= 1 - SLO = how much you're allowed to fail. 100% - 99.95% = 0.05% of 30 days = 21.6 minutes of allowed downtime per month.

🔎
Multi-Window Burn Rate Alerting — the Google Model

Alerting on a single threshold (e.g., error rate > 1%) produces false positives (a 60-second spike in errors isn't an incident). Google's multi-window model is more robust: alert when both a short window (1 hour) AND a long window (6 hours) show high burn rate simultaneously. This combines sensitivity (the 1-hour window catches fast-burning incidents quickly) with specificity (requiring the 6-hour window to also be elevated eliminates transient spikes).

Burn rate thresholds from Google SRE book:
Critical (page immediately): 1h burn rate >14.4× AND 5m burn rate >14.4× → will exhaust monthly budget in 2 hours
Warning (page during business hours): 6h burn rate >6× AND 1h burn rate >6× → will exhaust monthly budget in 5 days

3 · Prometheus — Pull-Based Metrics

🧠
Mental Model — Prometheus Scrapes; It Is Not Pushed To

Instead of your application sending metrics to a central server (push), Prometheus periodically requests metrics from your application's /metrics endpoint (pull). The advantage: if a target goes down, Prometheus immediately knows because the scrape fails. With push-based systems, a dead service just stops sending — and you have no signal. Pull-based systems make absence of data a detectable signal.

# ── Add /metrics to a FastAPI service ─────────────────────────
from prometheus_fastapi_instrumentator import Instrumentator
Instrumentator().instrument(app).expose(app)  # adds GET /metrics

# ── Tell Prometheus to scrape it (ServiceMonitor CRD) ─────────
apiVersion: monitoring.coreos.com/v1
kind: ServiceMonitor
metadata:
  name: payment-service
  labels:
    release: prometheus
spec:
  selector:
    matchLabels:
      app: payment-service
  endpoints:
    - port: http
      path: /metrics
      interval: 15s

Essential PromQL — The Queries Every SRE Knows

# Error rate (% 5xx) — the most important dashboard query
sum(rate(http_requests_total{status=~"5.."}[5m])) by (service)
/
sum(rate(http_requests_total[5m])) by (service) * 100

# p99 latency — what the 99th percentile user experiences
histogram_quantile(0.99,
  sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket[5m])) by (le, service)
)

# OOMKills — pods getting memory-killed
kube_pod_container_status_last_terminated_reason{reason="OOMKilled"}

# CPU throttling — pods hitting CPU limits (causes latency spikes)
rate(container_cpu_throttled_seconds_total[5m]) / rate(container_cpu_usage_seconds_total[5m]) * 100

# Disk pressure — nodes running out of disk
(node_filesystem_size_bytes - node_filesystem_avail_bytes)
/ node_filesystem_size_bytes * 100 > 80

5 · Structured Logging — The Foundation

Unstructured logging — impossible to query at scale
# Cannot filter by user_id, amount, or error_code
logging.error(
  f"Payment failed for user {user_id}: {str(e)}"
)
# Log: "Payment failed for user usr_123: Card declined"
# To find all failures: grep (slow, brittle, error-prone)
Structured JSON logging — queryable in ELK/Loki/Datadog
# Every field queryable, filterable, aggregatable
log.error("payment.failed",
  user_id=user_id, amount=amount,
  error_code=error.code,
  stripe_request_id=response.id,
  trace_id=current_span.trace_id,
  duration_ms=duration_ms,
)
# Kibana: error_code='card_declined' AND amount>10000
# → Find all high-value declines in 2 seconds, not 2 hours

7 · The On-Call Engineer's 5-Minute Framework

🧠
Alert → Triage → Mitigate → Investigate → Fix → Post-mortem
  1. Is it real? Is the alert flapping? Is this a known false positive? Rule out synthetic test failures.
  2. What's the impact? How many users affected? Which features are broken? Is it total or partial outage?
  3. Metrics: Which services show degradation on dashboards? Since when (compare to deployment times)?
  4. Logs: What errors appear at that timestamp? What's the error_code? Is there a common trace_id?
  5. Traces: Find a slow trace — which service/call is the bottleneck?
  6. Correlate: Did a deployment happen before the incident? Check deployment history first.
  7. Mitigate first: Rollback deployment? Toggle feature flag off? Enable circuit breaker? Restore service before finding root cause.
  8. Root cause later: During business hours. Don't debug at 3 AM — mitigate and sleep.
  9. Post-mortem: Blameless. Focus on systemic improvements. What monitoring would have caught this earlier?
🌍
Real-World: Google's Monarch — 1 Trillion Data Points per Minute

Google's internal metrics system (Monarch) processes 1 trillion data points per minute across all Google services. Their distributed tracing system (Dapper) was the inspiration for Jaeger, Zipkin, and the OpenTracing standard. The core insight from Google's SRE book: "The most important feature of a monitoring system is that it answers: 'Are things working? If not, what's wrong? If I need to fix it, how do I do so quickly?'" Observability is not about dashboards — it is about the speed at which you can answer those three questions under pressure at 3 AM.

✅
Chapter Takeaways
  • Metrics answer "what", logs answer "why it happened", traces answer "where the slowness is" — you need all three
  • Correlation via trace_id reduces MTTR from hours to minutes
  • SLOs with error budgets are the rational framework for trading feature velocity vs reliability
  • Multi-window burn rate alerting (Google model) reduces false positives dramatically
  • Structured JSON logs are a prerequisite for meaningful log analysis at scale
  • Mitigate first, root-cause second — don't debug at 3 AM, restore service and sleep
  • Post-mortems must be blameless and focus on systemic improvements
